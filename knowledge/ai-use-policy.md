# AI-use policy

The capstone follows the syllabus AI policy.

- **Allowed:** using AI to clarify concepts, debug code you understand, brainstorm, or generate practice questions.
- **Not allowed:** uploading private or personal datasets to generative-AI tools; submitting work substantially generated by AI without critical evaluation; copying AI output blindly.
- **Required:** a clear AI-use declaration in **every** deliverable (report, slides, proposal, contribution statement). Not declaring counts as an academic-honesty violation.
- **Accountability:** each student must be able to explain, defend and reproduce the work without AI during the defence. The quizzes, live presentations and oral defence exist to check this.

**Declaration shape** (end of report and slides): name the tools, say what they were used for, state that the team reviewed and owns all design decisions, analysis and writing, and confirm no private or personal data went into any AI tool.

The lecturer applied this concretely in Prosit 1: the proprietary household-survey data must not be uploaded to any AI tool, but asking AI to help understand the column structure (from the data dictionary) was fine. Open datasets had no such limit, so check each dataset's licence first.

Academic honesty more broadly: no solutions or code copied from peers, the internet or AI without declaring it.

## For the capstone
Our data are open (Groundsource CC BY 4.0, satellite/reanalysis products), so the "private data" rule is easy to meet, but keep a running log of AI use (e.g. in the repo) so the final declarations are accurate.

**What the lecturer said in class (lecture 9).** He removed the AI restriction from his project slide and gave a "green light" to use AI in the project, *because* he will drill each of you in the presentation. Use it as ethically and as much as you like, but you must understand and be able to explain everything. For the literature review: use AI only as a scaffold (ideas, candidate papers, checking code), never to write the piece; you are responsible for every sentence and citation; confirm every AI-suggested paper actually exists; if the writing does not sound like you, he may call you in to defend it. In lecture 11 he added that commits should carry a declaration of how AI was used.
